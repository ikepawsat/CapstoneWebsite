How Does Our Model Work?

Data

We used data gathered from Brian Rock and his marathon predictor. Since his predictor did not focus on individual applicants or any machine learning algorithm on them, we hoped that we could generate a better and more accurate cutoff time. We used his data from the Boston 2025 and 2026 races and qualifing windows. Additionally, we scrapped data from 2012-2019 off of the Boston Marathon website to create a list of prior runners. Other small datasets were collected but not widely used throughout the project.

Model

We tested dozens of models but ultimately ended up using a simple Gradient Boosting algorithm. The small complexity and small list of features meant that a simplier model worked the best for us. The next two best models for us was logit and a simple neural network. This model was created to assign a probability of applying to each applicant, which was then used to create the cutoff time. By creating multiple instances of the model, we were also able to produce a confidence interval. Because the confidence interval is both low and high, we take out the top and bottom 2.5% and make that our confidence interval. This means that we would be 95% sure that the cutoff time would have fallen in this range. Some other models we created were a model to predict applicants from the change in qualifiers year over year, a model that predicted cutoff time from the number of applicants rejected, and a model that creates a "pressure-score" value based on the previous year's cutoff time.

Prediction

We have created our model that assigns individuals a "likelihood to apply" value. The next step is to place applicants in order, from the highest buffer to the slowest. If an individual has a higher buffer time it means they were very far under the cutoff. For example, If a 21 year old male ran a 2:50 marathon and the cutoff was 2:55, they would have a 5 minute "buffer time". It is important to note that we use their probably to apply to determine whether they would apply. For example, if our threshold to apply is 60%, if they had a 55% chance, they would not apply. This creates our applicant number, which we then use to create our cutoff time. If the expected number of applicants was 32,000, we draw the line at 32,000 individuals and whatever that buffer time is becomes our projected cutoff.

Conclusions

What Went Wrong?

We believed that we could determine applicants from qualifiers; however, there was a big issue. The first was incomplete data. Our data was missing about 7,000-8,000 Boston Marathon runners. This is extremely important because the only way to know that a person applied to the Boston Marathon was to see if they ran the Boston Marathon. Additionally, this was just a difficult task. We knew we only had two years of data and one had to be our test set. Further, the lack of clarity from the Boston Athletic Association (the people who organize the marathon) hindered our knowledge on the behind the scenes process. This lack of clarity has always been a complaint, and is why we wanted to be able to project a cutoff time.

Future Work

In the future we would like to predict the change in qualifiers instead of predicting individual applicants. This would allow us to look at more years of data and get rid of the data issues mentioned above. Additionally, if we were to predict applicants, we would try to predict the correct number of applicants, rather than the correct individual. This would prioritize recall, which is something we tried to do, just did not focus on enough. If your model can predict an accurate applicant number, it is easy to predict an accurate cut off time. Currently, we are developing a model that predicts the cutoff time from months out. Our goal is to generate cutoff times from the start of the cutoff period to the end. If we see that there are more or less qualifiers than years prior, we can then adjust our predicted cutoff time to reflect to the change in qualifiers.
